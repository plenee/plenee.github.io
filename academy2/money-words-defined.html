The words Plenee uses

Plenee names a few things differently from the rest of the industry. Here is what each

one means, so a screen in the app and a line on your statement line up.

FLOW
money moving. Not what you own, not what you earn on paper. What actually arrived and actually left, in a period you choose.
inFLOW
money that arrived. Wages, benefits, a refund, a gift, a sale.
outFLOW
money that left. Every payment, whatever it was for.
coreFLOW
the part of outFLOW you cannot switch off this month. Rent or mortgage, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, food. The floor beneath your spending.
lifeFLOW
the rest. Everything you chose and could choose differently.
loanFLOW
payments that shrink what you owe. The principal part of a loan payment is loanFLOW; the interest part is a cost. This is why a big debt payment is not the same as a big expense.
saveFLOW
money moved into something you still own. A transfer to savings is not spending, even though it leaves your account.
NET
the verdict on a period. Did you come out ahead.
Net Plus
you ended the period ahead. This is the gap where wealth forms.
Net Minus
you ended behind. Sometimes a problem, sometimes the plan working, which is what retirement is.
Net Zero
you broke even.
NEST
what you actually own. Everything you hold, minus everything you owe.

What you owe

Principal
the amount borrowed, before interest. Paying principal reduces the debt; paying interest does not.
Interest
the price of borrowing, charged on the principal still outstanding.
APR (annual percentage rate)
the yearly cost of a loan including fees, not only the interest rate. Two loans can share an interest rate and differ in APR, and the APR is the one to compare.
Amortization
the schedule that splits each payment between interest and principal. Early payments are mostly interest, which is why five years into a thirty-year mortgage you have paid a great deal and still owe nearly all of it.
Term
how long you have to repay. A longer term lowers the monthly payment and raises the total cost, always.
Balloon payment
a large final payment at the end of a loan whose earlier payments were too small to clear it.
Deficiency balance
what you still owe after the lender has taken and sold the thing you borrowed against. Repossession does not end the debt.
Secured and unsecured
a secured debt is backed by property the lender can take. An unsecured debt is not, which is why it usually costs more.
Revolving credit
a limit you can borrow against repeatedly, like a credit card.
Installment credit
a fixed sum repaid on a fixed schedule, like a car loan.
Utilization
how much of your available credit you are using. It moves your credit score more than almost anything else you control month to month.
Grace period
the window in which paying a credit card in full costs no interest. It ends the moment you carry a balance, and does not resume until you clear it.
Minimum payment
the least the lender will accept without penalty. Paying it is not the same as making progress.

What things cost

Fee
a charge for a service, or for a transaction, or for existing. Read which.
Flat fee
a fixed amount, not a percentage. Usually the honest kind, because it does not grow when your balance does.
Basis point
one hundredth of one percent. A hundred basis points is one percent. Fees are quoted this way because it makes them sound small.
Expense ratio
the yearly percentage an investment fund charges you, taken out whether it made money or lost it.
Assets under management (AUM) fee
a percentage of everything you hold, charged every year. On a large balance this is a five-figure annual purchase, and it deserves an itemized bill.
Load
a sales commission on an investment, charged when you buy it or when you sell.
Commission
what the seller earns for selling. It is not a fee you pay separately; it comes out of what you handed over.
Markup
the difference between what something cost the seller and what it costs you. Common on products sold at a desk rather than shopped for.
Loss ratio
of the money customers pay an insurer, the share that comes back as claims. High is good for the buyer.

Insurance

Premium
what you pay for the cover.
Deductible
what you pay before the insurer pays anything. A percentage deductible on a home policy is not a flat sum, and can be several thousand dollars.
Claim
a request for the insurer to pay.
Rider
an add-on to a policy, priced separately, sometimes annually and for life.
Underwriting
the insurer deciding whether to cover you and at what price.
Exclusion
what the policy does not cover. The exclusions list decides what a policy is actually worth, and it is never in the brochure.
Actual cash value
replacement cost minus depreciation. It is what you get if the policy does not say replacement cost, and it is usually much less.
Replacement cost
what it takes to replace the thing new, with no deduction for age.
Liability cover
the part that pays when you are responsible for harm to someone else. It is the half of a policy that protects against the losses with no ceiling.
Umbrella cover
liability protection that sits above the limits on your existing policies, paying only after those are exhausted.
Debt waiver
an agreement by a lender to forgive part of a debt in some circumstance. Sold like insurance, but in most of the United States it is not insurance and is not regulated as insurance.
Unearned premium
the part of a premium covering a period that never happened, because the policy or loan ended early. It is generally owed back to you, and in many states nobody sends it unless you ask.

Investing

Asset
anything you own that has value.
Allocation
how your money is split between kinds of investment. It matters more than which particular investments you pick.
Index fund
a fund that holds everything in a market rather than choosing. Cheap because nobody is being paid to choose.
Active management
paying someone to pick investments. The fee is certain; the outperformance is not.
Turnover
how often a fund buys and sells. High turnover creates costs and tax you pay without being asked.
Capital gain
the profit when you sell something for more than you paid. Taxed, and taxed differently depending on how long you held it.
Compounding
returns earning returns. It needs time far more than it needs cleverness.
Fiduciary
someone legally required to act in your interest rather than their own. Ask in writing whether the person advising you is one, because the answer is often no.

Tax and pay

Gross and net pay
what you earned and what arrived. The gap is withholding.
Withholding
tax taken from your pay before you see it. Too much withheld is not a gift you get in spring; it is a year of your money held for free.
Marginal rate
the tax rate on your next dollar earned.
Effective rate
the share of your whole income actually paid in tax. It is always lower than the marginal rate, and confusing the two causes real, avoidable mistakes.
Tax-deferred
taxed later, when you take the money out.
Tax-free
not taxed on the way out, having been taxed on the way in.
Pre-tax
taken from your pay before tax is calculated, which lowers this year's bill.

Banking

Available balance
what you can actually spend right now. Different from the balance shown, because some money is pending.
Overdraft
spending past zero, which the bank may allow and charge for.
NSF fee
charged when the bank refuses the payment instead. Being refused is not the cheaper outcome; the bill still exists and now sits with whoever you owed.
ACH
the system moving most direct deposits and bill payments. It takes days, which is why timing matters.
Escrow
money a third party holds on your behalf, usually for property tax and insurance inside a mortgage payment.
Statement period
the window a statement covers. On a credit card the closing date matters more than the due date, because that is when the balance gets reported.
