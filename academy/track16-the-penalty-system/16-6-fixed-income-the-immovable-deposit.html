Fixed Income

The Immovable Deposit

Alignment decided by your birthday

Social Security pays once a month, and the date is set by the recipient's date of birth:

- Birthday on the 1st–10th → second Wednesday - Birthday on the 11th–20th → third Wednesday - Birthday on the 21st–31st → fourth Wednesday - SSI → the 1st; beneficiaries claiming before May 1997 → the 3rd1

Read that against a rent obligation due on the 1st and something uncomfortable becomes visible. A recipient paid on the 3rd covers the 1st from two days of carry. A recipient paid on the fourth Wednesday — as late as the 28th — covers the following month's 1st from four days of carry. Both are fine.

A recipient paid on the second or third Wednesday — the 8th through the 21st — must hold rent money for three to four weeks, through an entire month of ordinary expenses, without spending it.

Same benefit, same rent, same discipline required of everyone. Whether the arrangement is comfortable or precarious was decided by a birthday, and no one involved chose it or was told about it.

Why this case is the hardest

Two structural features make fixed income the most exposed situation in this track:

One deposit, no smoothing. A biweekly worker who misjudges a month has another paycheck coming in fourteen days. A monthly recipient has a thirty-day gap in which nothing arrives. Every obligation in the month has to be sequenced off a single inflow, and an error cannot be corrected until the next one.

The income side has no lever at all. Every other household in this track has at least a theoretical option on the deposit date — change jobs, change pay frequency, add a second income. A Social Security payment date cannot be changed by request, negotiation, or hardship. It follows the birthday, permanently.

So the fix is entirely one-sided: the bills must move to the deposit, because the deposit will never move to the bills.

Worked case: $1,740 a month, paid on the third Wednesday

A retiree receives $1,740, deposited on the third Wednesday — the 15th to the 21st depending on the month. Obligations: rent $1,050 due the 1st, Medicare supplement $185 on the 5th, utilities $190 on the 8th, phone $65 on the 12th, a credit card minimum of $180 on the 13th. Total $1,670, against $1,740 of income. A margin of $70.

Every one of those five bills falls before the deposit. All of them are funded by the previous month's payment, which means the household must carry roughly $1,670 across the month-end without touching it — while living on $70 of slack.

What actually happens: the card payment on the 13th, two days before the deposit, is the one that misses. At a $35-fee bank that is $35 from the bank and $30 from the issuer, $65 against a $70 margin. The month is gone. The following month starts $65 behind, so the utility misses too.

Now apply the track in order:

- Move the card from the 13th to the 25th — after the deposit in every month. Cost: one call. The double fee disappears. - Move the phone from the 12th to the 24th. The utility to the 23rd where the provider allows it. - Ask the landlord about a due date of the 25th with rent paid a month ahead of schedule rather than a week behind it — sometimes refused, occasionally accepted, and worth the ask because it is the largest item. - Check the bank. With $70 of margin, a $50 buffer and a next-day grace period are worth more than any budgeting change available.

After the first two moves alone, the pre-deposit cluster falls from $1,670 to about $1,240, and the two bills most likely to trigger the double fee are on the safe side of the deposit. The margin is still $70 — nothing was earned, nothing was cut — but it is no longer being spent on fees.

The part that is not a math problem

Two realities deserve saying plainly, because leaving them out would make this chapter dishonest.

A $70 margin is not a margin. It is a rounding error against a single unexpected expense, and the moves in this chapter do not create room — they stop room being taken. That is genuinely valuable and it is not a solution to a fixed income that is too low, which is a different problem with a different answer (When Preparation Isn't Enough).

And the population least able to absorb a $65 miss is, on average, the one facing the most friction in avoiding it: phone menus, hold times, online portals that assume a smartphone and a password manager, and a benefit deposit that cannot be moved. The cheapest fix in personal finance is also the one most likely to be blocked by a phone tree. This is worth naming because it reframes the failure — a retiree paying these fees is not failing to act on advice; they are frequently being defeated by the interface.

Income context: this case has no high-income counterpart. A retiree with substantial assets outside Social Security has a buffer, so the deposit date is irrelevant to them. The birth-date lottery only has consequences for people living close to the benefit amount.

Where Plenee fits

The deposit is perfectly regular and perfectly predictable — a single monthly amount on a rule-based date — which makes it the easiest inflow in the corpus to project. Plenee can show the entire month ahead with the trough marked, identify precisely which obligations sit on the wrong side of the deposit, and rank them by what each one costs when it misses. For a household making one decision a month with no room for error, one accurate forward calendar is worth more than any analysis performed after the fact.

The takeaway

If your income arrives on one fixed date you cannot change, every fix has to happen on the bills. Move the credit card first — it is the one that charges twice — then the phone and utilities, aiming for three to five days after your deposit. Ask about a split or a shifted date on the largest bill even if you expect a no. And check your bank's buffer and grace period: on a margin this thin, those two numbers matter more than anything you can do with a budget.
