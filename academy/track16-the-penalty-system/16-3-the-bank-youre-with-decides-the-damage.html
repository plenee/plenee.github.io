The Bank You're With Decides the Damage

The same mistake, priced from $0 to $108

Take one household, one bad morning, three autopays against a short balance. Hold everything constant — same income, same bills, same error — and change only where they bank:

- Capital One, Citibank, Ally, Alliant Credit Union, Discover, EverBank: $0. These institutions have eliminated overdraft fees entirely. - Bank of America: $20. $10 a fee, two a day. - Wells Fargo: $105. Regions: $108. U.S. Bank: no daily cap at all.

Nothing about the household differs. The entire spread — zero to over a hundred dollars, repeated monthly — is an artefact of an institution most people chose for its branch location, a signing bonus, or because it is where their parents banked.

For a household in the danger zone, which bank holds the checking account is a larger financial decision than any budgeting change available to them. That sentence is unusual in personal finance and it is not an exaggeration: no realistic spending adjustment on a $200 monthly margin moves $1,260 a year.

Read the whole schedule, not the headline number

Four features decide what a bank actually costs you, and only the first is advertised:

The fee. $0 to $36.

The daily cap. The multiplier. A $15 fee with a 3-a-day cap ($45) is worse on a bad morning than a $36 fee capped at 1 ($36). U.S. Bank's absence of a cap is the outlier worth avoiding on a clustered due-date calendar.

The buffer. A dollar threshold below which no fee is charged — Santander $100, Huntington and U.S. Bank $50, KeyBank $20. A $50 buffer eliminates the entire category of small-miss fees, which are a large share of real events.

The grace period. Time to cure before the fee sticks — Huntington until midnight the next business day, Wells Fargo and U.S. Bank 24 hours, Regions to the end of the next business day. For a household whose deposit lands the next morning, a 24-hour grace converts almost every gap into a non-event.1

A bank with a $50 buffer and a next-day grace is, for a thin-margin household, close to functionally equivalent to a $0-fee bank — and considerably easier to find with a branch.

The relief went to the people who needed it least

Look again at which institutions are at $0: Capital One, Ally, Discover, EverBank, Alliant. With one exception they are online or branchless. The institutions still charging $35–$36 are the branch networks.

Now ask who most needs a branch. Households paid partly in cash. Households who deposit paper checks and need the funds today. Households without reliable broadband, or without the documentation an online-only account application demands. Households who have had a problem that required a person to fix it.

That is a description of the same population the fees fall hardest on — and it is precisely the population least able to bank at the institutions that stopped charging. Fee relief has been concentrated among customers who rarely triggered the fee. No conspiracy is required to produce this; it falls out of the business models. But the effect is that the reform widely reported as fixing the problem largely bypassed the households with the problem.

Who actually pays

The distribution is not a gentle skew. It is a concentration:

- Under 9% of accounts generate close to 80% of all overdraft revenue. - Households paying 10 or more overdrafts a year average $380 a year in overdraft fees alone. - The median account balance in that group is under $350. - 34% of households earning under $65,000 were charged an overdraft or NSF fee last year, against 10% of households earning over $175,000.2

Read the second and third points together. The people paying $380 a year hold a median balance under $350 — the annual fee exceeds the balance it is charged against. This is not a fee on money. It is a fee on not having money, levied at the moment of not having it.

Income context: this is the entire reason this track exists and the reason it declines to spend chapters on high-margin optimisation. A household with $2,200 of monthly slack that leaves cash idle forgoes a few dollars of interest. A household with $120 of monthly slack that banks at Regions can lose its entire annual margin to one recurring bad morning. Same mechanism, incomparable stakes.

Nobody is coming to fix this

Two federal rules would have capped both halves of this system. Both died in 2025, twenty-five days apart.

The CFPB finalised a rule on 12 December 2024 capping overdraft fees at $5 for institutions holding over $10 billion in assets, effective 1 October 2025. Congress repealed it under the Congressional Review Act — Senate 52–48 on 27 March 2025, House 217–211 on 9 April — signed into law on 9 May 2025 as P.L. 119-10. The CRA additionally bars the CFPB from issuing a substantially similar rule without fresh authorisation from Congress.3

The credit card late fee cap of $8 was vacated by a federal court on 15 April 2025, on a consent judgment the CFPB joined.4

Both fees now stand at pre-rule levels, and one of them is legislatively fenced off from future action. The current structure is durable, not transitional. This matters practically: there is no version of waiting this out. The household-level fix — which bank, which dates — is the only lever that exists, which is what the rest of this track is about.

Where Plenee fits

Plenee already holds the fee transactions, which means it can do something no budgeting exercise can: total what a household's current bank charged it last year, and compare that against what the same twelve months of events would have cost at a $0-fee institution or one with a $50 buffer. That converts "should I switch banks" from a chore with no visible payoff into a number. And because the fee events are dated, it can also say which of them a buffer would have absorbed and which needed a date moved instead (Moving the Dates).

The takeaway

Check your bank's fee, its daily cap, its buffer, and its grace period — four numbers, all published, most people know none of them. If you carry a thin margin and your bank charges $35 with a 3-a-day cap and no buffer, switching institutions is very likely the highest-value financial action available to you this year, worth more than any spending change you could sustain. And do not wait for the rules to fix it: the two that would have died in 2025, and the overdraft one cannot easily return.
